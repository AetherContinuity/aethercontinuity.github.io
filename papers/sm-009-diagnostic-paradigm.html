The ACI Diagnostic Paradigm

What Cannot Be Measured Cannot Be Governed — A Unified Framework for Institutional Blindspot Analysis

This memo synthesises the intellectual arc of ACI's working paper series WP-001 through WP-018. The series began as an empirical study of energy system resilience and developed, through successive analytical iterations, into a general theory of institutional measurement failure. The paradigm can be stated in one sentence: when a system cannot measure a critical variable, it cannot sanction its deterioration, and therefore cannot correct it. This mechanism — measurement gap → sanction gap → correction gap — operates across sectors, scales, and institutional cultures. ACI has documented it in energy infrastructure (WP-001–WP-015, WP-017–WP-018), health data systems (WP-016), and financial market signalling (WP-017). The paradigm is not sector-specific. It is a structural property of complex governed systems in which the cost of measurement appears before the cost of its absence.

§ 01

The Origin: A Question About Time

ACI's first working paper (WP-001, 2026) asked a precise empirical question: how long can a high-electrification power system sustain household and critical infrastructure loads under compound winter stress without external resupply? The answer — expressed as Duration Adequacy — was a function of three variables: installed capacity, consumption rate, and the temporal duration of the stress event.

The question seems technical. Its implications are not. Duration adequacy is not primarily an engineering problem. It is a measurement problem. A system that does not measure its own temporal endurance cannot know when it is approaching its limit. And a system that does not know when it is approaching its limit cannot act in time to prevent the limit from being reached.

This was the first statement of what would become ACI's central paradigm: the gap between what a system can measure and what it needs to measure to govern itself adequately.

WP-001 foundational insight

Critical systems fail not because they exhaust their physical capacity, but because they exhaust their decision capacity first. The decision to act requires measurement of the approaching limit — and if that measurement does not exist, the decision arrives too late or not at all.

§ 02

The Development: From Physics to Institutions

Phase I — Physical measurement (WP-001–WP-005)

The early papers established the empirical baseline: what can be measured about energy system resilience, and what cannot. Duration adequacy (WP-001), distributed resilience doctrine (WP-002), compound stress modelling (WP-005) — all share the same structure. They ask: what does the system know about itself, and is that sufficient for it to avoid failure?

Phase II — Institutional architecture (WP-003, WP-008–WP-011)

WP-003 introduced Institutional Termination Time — the maximum interval between signal recognition and corrective decision before the opportunity to correct is lost. This concept elevated the measurement problem from the physical to the institutional level. A physical system may have adequate duration; the institution governing it may not have adequate decision time. The two can diverge — and when they do, the institution fails even if the system has not yet physically failed.

WP-008 extended this to allocation: why does capital flow to certain investments and not others? The answer was that allocation follows what is measured. If the measurement framework does not include long-horizon capacity integration, capital does not flow there — not because investors are irrational, but because the signal does not exist. WP-011 named the governance consequence: without a meta-coordinating function (System 4 in Beer's terminology), no actor in the system can correct the measurement gap from within.

Phase III — The intervention window (WP-012–WP-015)

WP-013 introduced the two-scenario framework: one in which institutional action continues at historical tempo, one in which three identified corrections are implemented. The divergence between scenarios grows nonlinearly — which means the cost of delay is not linear but accelerating. WP-014 formalised this as a dynamic coupled risk model, producing probability distributions over system states conditional on policy choices. WP-015 documented the mechanism producing the crisis: three simultaneous structural shifts — demand rigidification, supply buffer erosion, spot market residualisation — interacting to produce an elasticity collapse.

Phase IV — The general paradigm (WP-016–WP-018)

WP-016 (HDCI), WP-017 (bond spreads), and WP-018 (IQS) established that the measurement gap is not sector-specific. The Finnish health system does not measure integration quality, only data volume and cost. Sovereign credit markets do not measure capacity investment quality, only fiscal flow. Energy investment pipelines are indistinguishable to bond pricing regardless of their systemic integration quality. In each case, the same structure: a variable that matters is not measured; because it is not measured, deterioration produces no sanction; because there is no sanction, correction does not occur.

§ 03

The Paradigm: Three Gaps

The ACI Diagnostic Paradigm:

Measurement gap → Sanction gap → Correction gap → Structural decay

A critical variable is not measured.
Because it is not measured, its deterioration produces no observable signal.
Because there is no signal, no sanction is applied to the actor responsible for the variable.
Because there is no sanction, no correction occurs.
The variable continues to deteriorate until its absence produces a forcing event.
The forcing event imposes the cost that measurement would have prevented — at a time when the cost of correction is higher and the window for correction is narrower.

The paradigm has four structural properties that distinguish it from simple policy failure or political neglect:

1. It is self-concealing. The absence of measurement produces no visible signal. A health system that does not measure integration failure generates no alarm — the alarm requires the measurement to exist. A bond market that does not price capacity deficits generates no spread widening — the signal requires the model to include the variable. The failure mode is invisible precisely because the instrument that would make it visible does not exist.

2. It is self-reinforcing. Actors optimise for what is measured. When integration quality is not measured, actors optimise for installed volume — which is measured. When parliamentary decision latency is not priced by bond markets, politicians face no market discipline for D-suppression — which is priced. The measurement gap creates the incentive structure that produces the gap's consequences.

3. It is cross-sectoral. The paradigm does not depend on the specific content of the unmeasured variable. Energy integration quality, health care coordination quality, long-horizon investment quality — all produce the same structural consequence when unmeasured. This cross-sectorality is not a coincidence; it reflects the common structure of governed complex systems in which measurement is institutionally produced and politically mediated.

4. It has a temporal signature. The gap between measurement absence and forcing event varies by domain. In energy systems, compound stress accumulates over years. In health systems, integration failure accumulates across care episodes over months and years. In financial markets, capacity deficits accumulate until they manifest in GDP or fiscal aggregates — a 5–10 year horizon. The temporal signature determines how much advance warning measurement would provide — and therefore what the cost of measurement absence is.

Core paradigm finding

The cost of measurement is paid once, at the moment of instrument construction. The cost of measurement absence is paid repeatedly, in the form of suboptimal allocation, foregone correction, and eventual forcing events. The ratio of these costs grows nonlinearly with time — which is why measurement gaps that appear minor in stable periods become structurally critical during stress convergence.

The three instruments are structurally homologous. Each constructs a new observable quantity from existing data sources. Each makes visible a dimension of system quality that was previously unmeasured. Each creates the precondition for sanction — because sanction requires a measurable target, and the target did not previously exist.

None of the three instruments is sufficient alone. HDCI without a sanction mechanism is a diagnostic without a remedy. Bond spread analysis without an alternative instrument is a negative result without a prescription. IQS without outcome validation is a pilot without proof. The paradigm requires all three components: measurement, sanction design, and correction pathway.

§ 05

The Intellectual Arc: What Changed

The WP series began with a narrow empirical question about energy system temporal endurance. It arrived at a general theory of institutional measurement failure. This is not a coincidence — it is the predictable result of following an empirical anomaly rigorously.

The anomaly was simple: Finnish energy system risk was visible in technical analyses but not in institutional responses. Duration adequacy was calculable but not calculated by institutions. Compound stress scenarios were modelable but not modelled in policy. The intervention window was identifiable but not acted upon. Why?

The first answer was political: D-suppression, the three-stage filter through which risks fail to become decisions. But this is descriptive, not explanatory. D-suppression is the phenomenon; the paradigm asks what produces it structurally. The answer arrived through WP-017: D-suppression operates in part because the external sanction that would punish it — bond market pricing of capacity deficits — does not exist. Rating models do not price the unmeasured variable. Politicians face no external discipline for the gap they are not filling.

WP-016 confirmed this from a different domain. Finland's health system does not measure integration quality. Welfare areas face no sanction for integration failure. The governance structure rewards what it measures — cost and volume — and is indifferent to what it does not measure — integration and coordination. The structural homology with the energy case is precise.

WP-018 closed the circle: if bond spreads cannot distinguish between Finland's unintegrated wind capacity (IQS 4.1) and Denmark's integrated Ørsted system (IQS 8.6), then the financial market provides no signal to guide capital allocation toward integration quality. The market failure and the governance failure are the same failure, expressed in different institutional vocabularies.

§ 06

What the Paradigm Does Not Claim

Precision requires stating what the paradigm does not claim, to prevent overextension.

It does not claim that measurement solves the problem. Measurement is necessary but not sufficient. HDCI without a funding adjustment mechanism is a better diagnostic but not a better system. IQS without a policy anchor requirement is a more accurate map but not a changed landscape. The paradigm identifies measurement as the first condition for correction — not as correction itself.

It does not claim that all policy failure is measurement failure. Political resistance, distributional conflict, institutional path dependence, and coordination failure all operate independently of measurement gaps. The paradigm identifies one structural mechanism; it does not claim to be the only mechanism.

It does not claim that the instruments are ready for deployment. HDCI requires Findata licensing and empirical calibration. IQS requires inter-rater reliability and outcome validation. Bond spread analysis has produced a null finding, not an alternative instrument. The paradigm is an analytical framework; the instruments are in various stages of development.

It does not claim universality across all complex systems. The paradigm has been documented in three domains across a small-state institutional context. Generalisation to other institutional cultures, scale levels, and governance architectures requires additional validation.

§ 07

Research Programme

The paradigm generates a research programme with three active tracks:

Track A — Instrument completion

HDCI: Findata pilot (DT-006, Pohjois-Savo). IQS: inter-rater reliability, weight calibration, case expansion (Spain, Poland, Norway, Germany). Bond spread: term spread and CDS analysis, content analysis of rating statements (Salazar mechanism criterion A).

Track B — Sanction mechanism design

HDCI: funding adjustment model (§07 WP-016) — requires legislative pathway through STM. IQS: policy integration obligation as condition of grid connection (DT-004 additionality argument). Bond spreads: the research question is inverted — not "do spreads detect D-suppression?" but "what instrument would detect it, and what governance change would make that detection actionable?"

Track C — Cross-domain generalisation

The paradigm has been documented in energy, health, and finance. Candidate next domains: education system integration quality (analogous to HDCI — data rich, integration unmeasured), labour market skill transition quality (analogous to IQS — investment in training occurs, systemic utilisation unmeasured), municipal financial resilience (analogous to bond spread analysis — fiscal metrics available, structural capacity unmeasured).

Paradigm core statement

The question "why doesn't the system correct the problem it can see?" has a structural answer: the system does not measure the variable whose deterioration defines the problem. Without measurement there is no signal. Without signal there is no sanction. Without sanction there is no correction. The intervention that breaks this chain is not political will — it is measurement instrument construction. Political will follows measurement when measurement creates the conditions for accountability.

§ 08

The Third Sector Blind Spot

The three-gap paradigm applies with particular force to a domain that ACI's analysis has not yet addressed systematically: civil society organisations, foundations, and non-governmental organisations. These entities share a structural property that makes measurement failure not an accident but an architectural feature.

In commercial organisations, ownership is concentrated and owners have direct financial incentives to monitor management. In state organisations, democratic accountability provides at least a formal monitoring mechanism. In third-sector organisations, ownership is diffuse or absent: members are geographically dispersed and rarely engage in governance; funders measure outputs (beneficiaries served, programmes delivered) not inputs relative to outcomes (administrative cost ratios, executive compensation relative to mission impact); the public measures nothing because the data is not required to be public.

The result is a self-reinforcing structure. Governance bodies (boards, councils) are typically composed of individuals from the same professional network as the management they oversee. Transparency is voluntary, and voluntary transparency produces voluntary positive framing. Critical inquiry is discouraged by the implicit suggestion that questioning the organisation undermines its humanitarian or civic mission. The three gaps operate in sequence: administrative costs and executive compensation are not measured against mission impact (measurement gap); no external actor has standing to sanction underperformance or excess (sanction gap); the structure perpetuates itself across leadership generations (correction gap).

This structure scales. Across civil society organisations, labour union administrations, foundations, regional public bodies, and political party organisations, the same architectural pattern repeats: diffuse or absent ownership, voluntary transparency, internal governance by co-opted boards, and mission framing that discourages scrutiny. The specific institution does not matter — the pattern is the diagnostic subject. Where ownership is concentrated, measurement failures are eventually detected and corrected by the owner's self-interest. Where ownership is absent, measurement failures persist until an external forcing event — a media investigation, a funding crisis, a legal challenge — creates temporary visibility. Then the gap re-closes.

The diagnostic implication is that the measurement gap in third-sector governance is not a failure of individual actors but a structural property of organisations without residual claimants. Correcting it requires external mandatory disclosure — the equivalent of public company financial reporting requirements applied to organisations that receive public funding or tax benefits above a defined threshold. The instrument exists in other jurisdictions: in the United States, Form 990 requires all tax-exempt organisations to publicly disclose executive compensation, board composition, and financial ratios. No equivalent mandatory disclosure exists in Finland for third-sector organisations receiving public funding.

Structural Observation — Catch-22

The best place to conceal something is the place where no one looks. Third-sector governance is that place in Finnish institutional architecture. The paradigm — what is not measured is not sanctioned, what is not sanctioned is not corrected — operates most completely where ownership is most diffuse and transparency is most voluntary. Extending mandatory measurement to publicly-funded civil society organisations is the logical next application of the ACI diagnostic framework.

The correction mechanism faces a structural Catch-22. Reform requires measurement. Measurement requires a mandate. A mandate requires political will. Political will requires public pressure. Public pressure requires data. Data requires measurement. The circle closes. The state depends on civil society organisations for welfare services, crisis response, and voluntary sector capacity — and therefore has no incentive to impose transparency requirements that might disrupt those relationships. Donors measure the feeling of contribution, not administrative cost ratios. Members rarely attend governance meetings. The actor with the standing to require disclosure is the same actor that benefits from the absence of disclosure requirements. This is not a coordination failure that better information would resolve. It is a structural property of the system.

The structure also enables a particular career pattern in which individuals move between regulatory, political, and commercial roles in sequence — never present when the consequences of earlier decisions materialise, never absent when the decisions are made. No individual is ever wrong. No individual is ever responsible. The network absorbs accountability and redistributes it until it disappears. This is not corruption in the legal sense. It is the natural equilibrium of a system without residual claimants and without mandatory measurement.

The systemic cost of this structure extends beyond the organisations themselves. When governance failures in civil society organisations become visible — through investigative journalism, legal proceedings, or financial crises — the damage is not confined to the institution. Public trust in charitable giving and voluntary sector participation depreciates broadly. A donor who discovers that administrative costs or executive compensation consumed a significant share of their contribution does not simply redirect their giving to a better-managed organisation. They reduce their giving overall. This is the inflation of goodwill: the social capital that civil society depends on is consumed by the governance failures of individual actors, but the cost is distributed across the entire sector. Those who most depend on third-sector services — people in crisis, in poverty, in isolation — bear the cost of a governance failure they had no part in creating and no mechanism to prevent. The measurement gap does not only conceal costs. It destroys the resource base that makes the mission possible.

§ 09

The Sanction Gap in Practice: Capacity Mechanism as Case Study

The three-gap paradigm — measurement gap, sanction gap, correction gap — is sometimes presented as a theoretical construct. The Finnish capacity mechanism process provides a concrete, documented case of all three gaps operating in sequence over a multi-year period.

The measurement gap is well established: Fingrid has published annual system adequacy reports since at least 2021 documenting structural capacity risks, particularly under cold, calm winter conditions. The risk has been identified, quantified, and publicly communicated. This is the measurement gap closing — or appearing to close.

The sanction gap is where the process stalls. A proposal to reform the strategic reserve mechanism was made in 2021. The 2023 government programme listed capacity mechanism development as a target for study. In spring 2024, the responsible minister assessed that a government proposal would reach parliament "next year." In May 2026, no government proposal has been submitted. An electricity market law amendment entered into force on 1 January 2026 — it addressed grid development, not capacity mechanism. Three years of preparation have produced no legislative action.

The correction gap follows structurally: without legislative action, the underlying structural risk — CHP phase-out, inflexible data centre load, SE1 transmission dependency — continues to accumulate. Fingrid's three-tier alert system represents a reactive response to the gap, not a structural correction. It manages symptoms; it does not address causes.

A parallel case: the Mattila report on data centres (November 2025), commissioned by Prime Minister Orpo in June 2025, recommended that data centres be required to provide up to 20% demand flexibility during high-price hours. This recommendation has received no concrete follow-up. The report did not address ownership structures, transfer pricing, or the mechanisms by which data centre value is retained in Finland. Its commissioning satisfied the political requirement to have "addressed the issue" without requiring the political cost of implementation.

The paradigm identifies the structural property that explains both cases. Inaction does not carry a political price. The responsible ministry (TEM) has political accountability but lacks the instruments to act unilaterally — capacity mechanism legislation requires parliamentary process, which requires Treasury assessment, which confronts VM's structural preference for avoiding open-ended commitments. The result is a system in which studies are completed, reports are published, warnings are issued, and no correction occurs — because the correction mechanism requires coordination across institutional boundaries that the system is not designed to cross.

Empirical Observation

Three years of documented capacity risk, three rounds of study and assessment, zero government proposals submitted to parliament. This is not policy failure in the conventional sense — it is the predicted output of a system with a structural sanction gap. No actor faces consequences for inaction. No actor has sufficient authority and instruments to act alone. The paradigm does not predict when correction will occur. It predicts that correction will not occur until either the sanction gap closes — through a forcing event that makes inaction politically costly — or the system's institutional design changes to assign clear ownership of the aggregate risk.

§ 10

The Sanction Gap in Practice: Business Subsidies as Second Case Study

The capacity mechanism case study in §09 documents the measurement–sanction–correction gap in energy infrastructure policy. The Finnish business subsidy structure provides a second, independent empirical example of the same pattern operating simultaneously.

In 2025, Finland distributed 1.4 billion euros in direct business subsidies. Of this, 70% went to the TKI (research, development and innovation), internationalisation and business activity category. Energy subsidies totalled 171 million euros. Environmental protection received 4 million euros. The ratio — 171 million to 4 million — is not a technical outcome. It is a political choice. No actor is required to justify this allocation against system-level environmental or resilience outcomes. No measurement instrument captures the aggregate effect of subsidy distribution on energy system resilience. No sanction follows from the imbalance.

The concentration structure compounds this. In 2025, fewer than 14 companies received over half of all direct business subsidies. This is the same concentration risk documented in CN-009 for Denmark's Novo Nordisk and Ørsted dependence — located in a different sector, operating through a different mechanism, but producing the same measurement gap: high output quality from concentrated recipients, low diversification of the underlying support structure, no systematic monitoring of aggregate exposure.

The parallel to the capacity mechanism case is structural, not incidental. In both cases: the data is public, the pattern is visible, no institution is responsible for the aggregate outcome, and no political cost attaches to continuation. The subsidy structure that existed in 2020 largely persists in 2025 because the three-gap mechanism — no measurement of aggregate effect, no sanction for imbalance, no correction mechanism short of crisis — operates identically across both domains.

Empirical Observation — Business Subsidies

Environmental protection received 4 million euros in business subsidies in 2025; energy policy objectives received 171 million. TKI and internationalisation received over 1 billion. This allocation is not evaluated against energy system resilience, digital sovereignty objectives, or security-of-supply criteria in any systematic government assessment. No actor owns the aggregate. No sanction follows from the structure. The diagnostic prediction: this allocation will not change until a forcing event makes the imbalance politically costly.

§ 11

A Paradigm Variant: The Disclosure Gap (August 2026)

§§01–10 describe cases in which a critical variable is not measured at all. Data centre demand-flexibility capacity presents a structurally distinct variant: the variable is measured, under binding EU mandate — and is simultaneously sealed by law from the actors who would need it to act. This is not a measurement gap. It is a disclosure gap, and it produces the same downstream sequence — no signal, no sanction, no correction — through a different mechanism.

Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364, implementing Article 12 of the recast Energy Efficiency Directive (2023/1791), requires all data centres with installed IT power demand of at least 500 kW — enterprise, colocation, and co-hosting alike — to report 24 performance indicators annually to a common European database: energy consumption, water consumption, waste heat, capacity utilisation, and whether the facility provides network services, among others. The 2025 reporting year fell due 15 May 2026. Sweden's implementing statute (Lag 2025:570, in force 1 July 2025) confirms the obligation precisely — and, in the same section, confirms the seal: "Uppgifter i EU-databasen omfattas av sekretess enligt 30 kap. 23 § Offentlighets- och sekretesslagen (2009:400)" — data in the EU database is subject to secrecy under Chapter 30, Section 23 of the Public Access to Information and Secrecy Act. Reported data is public only in aggregated form, at member-state and EU level. The individual, facility-level figures — precisely the granularity a capacity-mechanism or demand-flexibility policy would need — are collected and legally inaccessible to the institutions that would use them.

This reframes the diagnostic question. It is not "why does no one measure data centre flexibility potential" — the measurement exists, mandated and funded by the same regulatory apparatus proposing to act on it. It is: why has a system that already collects the critical variable simultaneously legislated that variable out of decision-making reach. The paradigm's four structural properties (§03) apply with a sharper edge here: the gap is not an absence but an active legal construction, verifiable by a single question to a national energy authority and a single reading of a public-access statute — falsifiable in a way the general three-gap model, applied to less legally codified domains, is not.

Finland's version is the stronger case for "active legal construction," not a borrowed Swedish finding

Finland's own implementation of EED Article 12(1), HE 170/2024 vp (Government proposal to amend the Energy Efficiency Act, Minister Kai Mykkänen, prepared by TEM with LVM, Energiavirasto, Motiva, and Datakeskusyhdistys; ~50 data centres initially estimated in scope), states the public disclosure floor directly and precisely: "datakeskusten... tulee asettaa yleisesti saataville tieto sijaintimaakunnastaan. Lisäksi lakiin lisätään säännökset Energiaviraston oikeudesta tiedon saantiin ja tarkastuksiin" — data centres must make publicly available information about their host province (maakunta) only; the Energy Authority separately receives full access and inspection rights. This is a materially different construction from Sweden's: Sweden's seal (§ above) operates through a general business-secrecy statute (OSL 30:23§) that covers data-centre reporting as one instance among many categories of commercially sensitive information it protects — the boundary is a side-effect of general secrecy law, not a data-centre-specific choice. Finland's boundary — province-level public disclosure, full regulator access, nothing in between — is written into the specific data-centre statute itself, as an affirmative legislative decision about exactly how much the public gets to see. The disclosure gap this section describes is, in the Finnish case, a deliberate design choice rather than an incidental byproduct of unrelated confidentiality law — the stronger and more precisely falsifiable instance of the paradigm variant, not a weaker echo of the Swedish one.

A second, distinct gap: the waste-heat obligation itself is a low bar by the regulated industry's own account

A separate finding, adjacent to but distinct from the disclosure gap: the substantive obligation Finnish law places on data centres to utilise their waste heat is, by the account of Energiateollisuus (the Finnish energy industry association, in formal consultation responses on the energy efficiency law amendments to HE 85/2025, the later and distinct proposal from HE 170/2024 above — enacted as Energiatehokkuuslaki 1372/2025), easy to satisfy without meaningful action. Energiateollisuus's own submission states the requirement is markedly looser than for other industrial facilities, because a data centre can fulfil it by using only a very small share of its total waste heat purely internally — to heat itself — at which point no cost-benefit analysis is required at all. Energiavirasto's own official published description of the enacted obligation confirms the same mechanism independently, in its own words: a cost-benefit analysis demonstrating technical or economic infeasibility is required only "jos olemassa olevan datakeskuksen hukkalämpöä ei miltään osin hyödynnetä" — if the waste heat of an existing data centre is not utilised in any part at all. Any partial internal use, however small, satisfies the statute without triggering the test that would otherwise establish whether fuller, external utilisation is feasible. The regulator's own description and the regulated industry's critical account converge exactly, from two sources with no shared interest in agreeing. Energiateollisuus's own conclusion: the law does not require maximising waste-heat use even where doing so would be technically and economically feasible, and proposes tightening the exemption to apply only where a data centre's Energy Reuse Factor (ERF) exceeds 40%, specifically to ensure data centres are pushed toward broad waste-heat utilisation rather than minimal internal self-heating. This is not a disclosure gap — the obligation itself, and both the regulator's and industry's own accounts of its weakness, are public. It is the paradigm's sanction-gap component operating on a different variable: the requirement exists on paper, but is calibrated low enough that satisfying it carries no real behavioural consequence.

Unverified, flagged as such: it is tempting to read this low bar as evidence of direct data-centre-industry influence over the specific exemption clause — Datakeskusyhdistys (the Finnish Data Centre Association) is confirmed, by name, as part of the expert group that prepared the earlier, separate disclosure-requirement law (HE 170/2024 vp, §11 above). For HE 85/2025 specifically — the proposal containing the waste-heat exemption — the only confirmation found is Energiateollisuus's own generic acknowledgement of "eri sidosryhmiä osallistaneesta valmistelusta" (preparation involving various stakeholder groups), which does not name which groups. No source found confirms Datakeskusyhdistys's specific participation in drafting this particular clause. The connection is plausible — an organised industry association was formally involved in adjacent legislative preparation on the same general subject, and the resulting exemption favours that same industry — but it is an inference, not a demonstrated fact, and is recorded here as exactly that rather than folded into the finding above as if verified.

Context, not evidence of causation: the scale asymmetry between regulated firms and the regulating state

Whatever role any specific stakeholder played in drafting either statute, the resource asymmetry between the entities the disclosure and waste-heat obligations regulate and the state apparatus regulating them is verifiable and stark, independent of any claim about influence over a particular clause. Microsoft's revenue in a single reported quarter (2026) was $82.9 billion — approaching the entirety of Finland's 2026 state budget (~€55.5 billion) in one financial quarter of one company. Microsoft's own guidance for 2026 capital expenditure alone is $190 billion — more than three times Finland's full annual state budget. Across the four largest US cloud/AI infrastructure firms (Microsoft, Alphabet/Google, Amazon, Meta) combined, planned 2026 AI infrastructure spending is reported at $725 billion — on the order of twelve times Finland's annual state budget. This is not a new asymmetry: Alphabet's revenue already exceeded Finland's state budget by a factor of roughly 1.4 in 2016. The point this section draws from the comparison is not that resource scale proves influence over any specific legislative clause — it does not, and the inference above is marked unverified precisely because scale alone cannot substitute for a documented causal chain. The point is narrower and does not require proving causation: a regulatory apparatus operating at this scale disparity relative to the entities it regulates faces a structural capacity question — independent of intent on either side — about whether ongoing monitoring, enforcement, and iterative tightening of exemptions like the one above can realistically keep pace with the regulated firms' own capacity to adapt, litigate, or simply outlast the review cycle. That capacity question is itself a measurement-paradigm-relevant variable (§03's four structural properties), and is not currently tracked by any instrument in the ACI corpus.

The flexibility claim, tested against reported evidence

A separate, adjacent claim in public discussion — that data centres already provide meaningful grid flexibility, making further obligation unnecessary — does not survive contact with the same Nordic evidence base. Energiforsk report 2025:1089 ("Datacenter som flexibilitetskapacitet", Petersson, Stark & Gustafsson, RISE, February 2025), based on interviews with ten Swedish data centre owners, finds willingness but structurally limited and unevenly distributed capacity. Colocation data centres typically operate under strict contracts with their customers governing what they may and may not do with respect to flexibility services and energy-related measures — but this is not a uniform legal wall: some colocation operators defer entirely to customer preference, others act unilaterally and accept that dissatisfied customers may relocate. Cryptocurrency, rendering, and some HPC workloads are comparatively easier to flex; mainstream enterprise and hyperscale load faces a different constraint — the report finds compensation matters progressively less to larger operators, meaning the binding constraint for the largest facilities is economic incentive, not contractual restriction. Of the ten operators interviewed, 22% were actively using ancillary services at the time of interview and 44% had begun the onboarding process.

The report supplies MW-scale figures directly relevant to the inventory this section otherwise treats as sealed (Table 3, sourced from Radar Ecosystem Specialist / Energimyndigheten 2023, reference year 2020): installed and average power by data-centre type in Sweden — enterprise 319 MW installed / 77 MW average (24% utilisation); hyperscale 99 MW / 70 MW (71%); colocation 225 MW / 122 MW (54%); crypto 200 MW / 180 MW (90%). From this base the report derives two flexibility-participation scenarios: excluding hyperscalers, with roughly 20% of average load assumed flexible with modest technical measures, yields 75 MW; including hyperscalers, at roughly 40% participation, yields 180 MW. A worked example makes the volume point concretely: one Sweden-region-1 data centre prequalified 21.7 MW on the FCR-D Up market — yet delivered only 23 MWh of actual activated energy across the whole of 2023. Prequalified capacity and delivered energy are different quantities, and the gap between them is the same gap this section identifies between marketing claims and reported outcomes.

A further reason FCR-D specifically does not function as a scarcity-relevant signal, independent of activation frequency: the report documents that FCR-D compensation collapsed through 2024 as prequalified battery storage volume increased sharply, to roughly one-tenth of the 2023 monthly average by late 2024 — a straightforward oversupply effect in the ancillary-service market itself, not a data-centre-specific finding, but one that further weakens any argument that data centres are being paid a meaningful signal to provide FCR-D flexibility today. Separately, Svensk Näringsliv documents Vattenfall marketing backup-generator activation to data centres as a peak-shaving option — a commercial pitch, not a reported, realised capability; the same industry that receives the pitch does not publish data on whether or how often it is taken up. The distinction matters: "data centres have flexibility potential" (true, per interviews, and now quantified: 75–180 MW nationally under stated assumptions) and "data centres are already delivering flexibility at scale" (not supported by the same report's own activation and compensation data) are different claims, and public discussion routinely elides them.

No Nordic dispatchable-capacity obligation exists — and the reason is EU law, not policy inertia

Sweden, like Finland, currently relies on conditional grid-connection agreements rather than a binding production or flexibility mandate for large new loads — a null finding, not an oversight. Swedish law (as implemented via Riksdagen's regulatory framework) requires that grid operators may not impose technical requirements or conditions that impede demand flexibility; that flexibility services must primarily be procured on market terms; and that non-market methods such as conditional connection agreements may be used only where market-based procurement is unavailable or fully exhausted — and even then, the method used to construct such conditions must be approved in advance by the regulator (Ei). This is the same EU non-discriminatory-access constraint that any Finnish legislative proposal modelled on conditional obligations — including elements of the Vestman initiative (§4.3, WP-018) — will need to clear. The disclosure gap and the dispatchable-obligation gap are therefore linked at the EU-law level, not merely by shared subject matter: both are constrained by the same non-discrimination architecture that governs how, and whether, a member state may condition grid access on flexibility or production commitments.

Empirical Observation — Disclosure Gap

Facility-level data centre energy, water, waste-heat and capacity-utilisation data is collected under binding EU mandate (Delegated Regulation 2024/1364) and simultaneously sealed under national secrecy law (Sweden: OSL 30:23§) from public and institutional access beyond aggregate form. The measurement gap the wider paradigm describes as an absence is, in this case, a presence rendered inert by statute — a variant with a sharper falsifiability profile than the general model. Separately, Nordic evidence (Energiforsk 2025:1089) does not support the claim that data centres already deliver material grid flexibility at scale: contractual SLA constraints on colocation load and the low energy volume of the ancillary services data centres do provide (principally FCR-D, minutes-scale activations) both limit realised flexibility well below what marketing communications and general "data centres can flex" claims suggest. No Nordic country currently imposes a binding dispatchable-capacity or flexibility obligation on large new loads — a null finding constrained by the same EU non-discriminatory grid-access framework that governs conditional connection agreements generally.

Follow-up, resolved (August 2026): both open items above have now been checked. Energiforsk 2025:1089's MW-scale estimates are incorporated above. Svenska kraftnät submitted its own connection-process report on 30 April 2026 as instructed (government mandate, September 2025), covering production/consumption siting, long-term PPAs, connection incentives, and harmonised power requirements. Its most directly relevant finding: Svenska kraftnät is now preparing tools and routines to implement conditional connection agreements (villkorade avtal) as a mechanism for faster connection — the connecting party's counter-performance being an obligation to curtail offtake or feed-in when the grid risks overload. This is Sweden's own live move toward exactly the mechanism this section identifies as EU-law-constrained (market-based procurement first; non-market conditional agreements only where market procurement is unavailable or exhausted; method subject to regulator pre-approval). Stakeholder submissions to the same process explicitly requested faster large-customer connection and evaluation of conditional agreements for that purpose — confirming that the EU non-discrimination constraint identified above is a live, current design question in Sweden's own reform process, not a settled, historical matter. Finland's own autumn 2026 proposal (§4.3, WP-018) will need to navigate the identical constraint.
